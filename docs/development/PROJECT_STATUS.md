## The plan is now one month, and the target is quality

**Decided by the owner, 2026-08-15, after running the application themselves.**
Their words: the eyes are wrong far more often than they are right, a slight
turn nearly loses the face, and the quality only holds within five to ten
degrees of straight ahead. They also observed, correctly, that day 1 to day 2
improved a great deal and day 3 barely moved.

That observation is the reason for the change. The first two days removed
outright defects; what is left is the method's ceiling. Every expression axis
examined since has turned out not to survive being blended: the four gaze
photographs were dropped, the squint photograph was dropped, and two of
MediaPipe's eye blendshapes were found to measure something other than their
names. That is a pattern, not a run of bad luck.

**So: four weeks, and a neural refiner is the plan's centre.** Not a face
swapper — the licence-clean, entirely-ours version: a small network trained on
the owner's own generated clips to correct what the warp gets wrong. Its input
is an already-posed, already-correct face needing artefacts removed, which is a
far smaller problem than synthesising one, and B001 measured that shape at
**1.83 ms fp32 / 1.33 ms fp16** — it fits.

| Week | |
|---|---|
| **1** | The broken turn sources, the brows, and 20 fps. A repeatable quality measure. The training-pair generator. |
| **2** | First refiner. First honest comparison. |
| **3** | Iterate: architecture, data, temporal stability. |
| **4** | Integrate, optimise, `.exe` and the kill switch, deliver. |

**`.exe` at the end, by the owner's choice** — everything gets its proper time
and nothing is rushed.

**The odds, given plainly rather than promised:** roughly one in five that this
reaches "high level"; roughly two in three that it ends up markedly better than
today; about one in four that the network does not earn its place and the
improved warp is what ships. The largest risk is the gap between studio clips
and a real room, and it is smallest here because the network never sees the
camera — only the avatar, which is already normalised.

**A commitment made with it:** a fixed clip, fixed frames, one number, reported
before and after every change. Several times today the owner was told a defect
was found and still saw the same thing. A measure they can watch is worth more
than another assurance.

### Day 9 — the clips stop being generated by hand

**Decided 2026-08-16: buy API credit, not a subscription.** The remaining clip
list is finite and it is one face. Priced against it, roughly 14-18 generations
including retries, a $50 top-up covers the work where the flat plan is 250 GEL;
the break-even is around 30 generations. The plan wins only if the prompts keep
disobeying, and if that happens the answer is a different prompt, not more takes.

**What the money bought instead of a subscription: a loop that measures.**
`scripts/generate_avatar_clips.py` generates a clip, runs it straight through the
extractor, reads the measurement, and generates again only when the measurement
failed. Two clips have already been paid for twice because nothing measured them
until days later — a sweep whose every rung was a smear, and a two-sided take
that came back one-sided. Both of those are now a verdict within a minute of the
file landing, and the second one is caught by yaw sign rather than by eye.

* Prompts moved out of prose into `configs/avatar_clips.toml`, one entry per
  clip with the measurement that accepts it. `AVATAR_VIDEO_PROMPTS.txt` keeps
  the reasoning and points at `--print-prompt` for the text.
* `extract_avatar_frames.py --report` writes the same numbers it prints as JSON,
  and distinguishes a bad clip from a broken checkout — a missing model bundle
  must not read as "generate it again".
* Every request is priced before it is sent, refused if it would cross
  `--budget`, and written to a ledger the instant it returns. `--plan` prices a
  whole run and sends nothing. Turn clips buy a cheap probe first, because what
  has failed twice is prompt-following, which a third of the price can test.
* The turn prompts now ask for the movement *between* stops to be slow — the
  owner watched the stepped clip and the head snapped between positions. Seven
  steps rather than eight, so they fit eight seconds unhurried.
* 459 tests (was 430); the new ones cover the budget guard, the ledger, and
  every verdict the judge can reach.

**The owner then chose to generate by hand on the site and hand the files over,
so the API half is not being used yet.** The measuring half needs no key and no
SDK: `extract_avatar_frames.py` reads a downloaded file exactly the same way.
What the catalogue change bought regardless is the clip list itself — **eight
clips, three held positions each**, because the generator caps at eight seconds
and the old prompts asked for fifteen seconds of expressions inside them. A
generator resolves that by rushing every position, and a rushed position is a
blurred frame. The squint and the four gaze clips are not on the list at all:
both families were dropped from the engine, so their frames would be unread.

**Not yet done if the API route is taken:** none of it has been run against the
live service. The model ids and the per-second prices in the catalogue are
written down, not verified — `--list-models` asks the account what it actually
offers, and the billing page is the authority on price. A wrong rate there makes
the budget guard wrong in the same direction.

### Day 10 — one photograph per region, chosen rather than averaged

**The owner's report:** they smile, the teeth appear, and the closed mouth is
still there underneath at low opacity. Two photographs on screen at once.

**One cause for both halves.** A region mixed *every* photograph that scored
anything — at a wide smile the mouth was a smile photograph averaged with a
pressed-lips one and a rounded one — and whatever the mix did not claim was left
as base portrait, whose lips are shut. An average of pictures that disagree is a
picture of neither, and the remainder is a ghost of a closed mouth over an open
one. The mouth *interior* was fixed exactly this way on day 8; this extends the
same rule from the interior to the whole region.

* `_one_per_region` keeps the strongest photograph in each of mouth, eyes and
  brows and drops the rest. Whole-head photographs — the turn roles and the
  ladder's rungs — pass through untouched: a rung is not a rival of a mouth, it
  says what angle the head is at, and the ladder already chooses by that angle.
* `_commit` decides how much of the winner is applied. A straight line up to
  **0.70 of the expression** and flat at one after it, so past that point the
  base portrait contributes *nothing* to the region. That is the half of the
  complaint about opacity.
* `holding` on the loaded avatar carries the previous frame's winner, and a
  challenger has to beat it by **0.10** to take the region. Without that the two
  nearest expressions swap places every time their scores cross, which on a face
  mid-sentence is several times a second, and each swap is a hard cut. A region
  with no photograph at all forgets its incumbent, so the next expression
  arrives unopposed.
* 469 tests (was 459). The new ones state the complaint directly: only one
  photograph survives a region, a committed expression leaves nothing
  underneath, a narrow challenge does not take the region and a clear one does.

**What this does not do.** It chooses among the photographs the avatar has, one
per role. The 480 frames dumped from the six new clips cannot all be used yet:
the engine's expression slots are *named roles* with one photograph each, and
the only dense axis it owns is yaw, through the ladder. Using a library this
size needs that ladder generalised to the expression axes — nearest by lip gap,
by brow raise — which is the same shape of change and a real one.

**Also measured, on the mesh:** the tracker returns **478 landmarks and the warp
uses 139**. The brows get 6 points each, and the brows are the region that fits
worst at every angle (20.9 px of carry error at 39°). There is headroom there
without a new model.

**The teeth, verified on the owner's own driving clip rather than in a test:**
at the widest smile the teeth are fully opaque with no closed mouth behind them.
That is day 10's region change working on real footage.

**Then a second defect, found by driving the engine with a turn clip.** The
resting angle was seeded from the first frame and thereafter followed a mean so
slow it moved 0.2% of the error per frame. Driven by a clip that *opens* at
31.7°, the engine took that for straight ahead and chose a 30°-turned photograph
for a frontal face — a cheek stretched sideways, and half a minute before it
recovered.

* For the first **120 frames** the resting angle is a running mean with a
  prior of 8 frames: fast enough to correct an opening turn in four seconds,
  damped enough that one early frame cannot teach it that a turn is straight
  ahead. The first frame still returns exactly zero.
* After that it is the old slow mean, **weighted down by distance** — a reading
  12° from rest counts for half, 24° for a fifth. A glance held at a second
  monitor is no longer slowly learned as a new way of sitting, while someone who
  genuinely moves their chair still is.
* Measured on the clip that exposed it: the error at frame 80 fell from 31.5° to
  15.0°, and it keeps falling. The smear is much reduced and not gone, because
  that clip never sits still at all — there is no resting angle in it to find.
* 476 tests (was 469).

### Day 10, third pass — what the owner saw when they ran it

Five reports from live use. Three are fixed, two are measured and named.

**1. "When my teeth show it thinks I am laughing."** Correct, and it was the
library rather than the engine: the only smile-with-teeth photograph in it was
the laugh from the end of the smiles clip — mouth wide, eyes not smiling. The
extractor picks the *strongest* example of a role, and for a smile the strongest
is a laugh. Replaced from the same clip at 4.5 s, a warm open smile with the
upper teeth showing, and `source_smile_closed` from 2.2 s. Lip gaps 0.074 and
0.017, either side of the 0.055 that decides which photographs have an interior.

**2. "The teeth appear as if the frame had been cut."** The amount was read
straight off the tracker, and a blendshape crosses the commit point in a frame
or two, so a photograph correct for the mouth's final shape arrived whole while
the lips were still moving. `_eased` now rate-limits it: an eighth per frame,
eight frames from nothing to whole — 0.27 s at 30 fps. Only the *amount* is
limited. Which photograph it is is still decided instantly, because a fade
between two of them is the double exposure this path exists to remove.

**3. "Turning my head smears — dough, around the nose."** The ladder blended
whichever two rungs bracketed the angle, in proportion, however far apart they
were. That is right at the three degrees the extractor aims for and wrong at the
eight the second clip left between +11° and +19°, where the head was moving
rather than stopping: a cheek at +11 laid over a cheek at +19. The cross-fade
now keeps its width in *degrees* — three of them, in the middle of the gap — so
across a wide gap one photograph is shown whole and the change is quick. A
ladder at its intended spacing behaves exactly as before.

### Day 10, fourth pass — the mask at a turn, and regions that overlapped

**"The part next to my ear shows on the face — it does not cover me there."**
Measured before touching anything, on the turn clip, as the share of the live
face the mask leaves showing:

| yaw | before | after |
|---|---|---|
| 0-9° | 8.1% | **8.1%** |
| 10-19° | 7.7% | 7.5% |
| 20-29° | 7.2% | 4.5% |
| 30-39° | 6.9% | **1.7%** |
| 40-49° | 7.0% | **1.1%** |
| 50-59° | 7.7% | 1.2% |

The mask is eroded by 4% of face width all the way round so the join happens
over skin on both sides rather than over the background. Frontal that is right:
the edge it pulls back from is the silhouette, and outside it are hair and room.
Turned, the near side's edge runs down in front of the ear and what lies outside
*that* is more cheek — so there the erosion is pure loss, a band of the owner's
real face left showing inside their own avatar, at the widest part of the turn.

The near side now keeps its edge, fading in from about fifteen degrees and fully
by thirty-five. The turn is read from the mesh rather than from the tracker's
pose — the nose's offset between the two edges of the outline, measured at 0.14
frontal, 0.42 at 10-19°, 0.69 at 20-29°, 0.89 at 30-39° — so the mask and the
picture it is drawn in can never disagree about which cheek faces the camera.
The restored piece is clipped to the outline first, so it can only undo the
erosion and never reach into hair. **Frontal behaviour is unchanged**, which the
table's first row is there to hold.

**"Sometimes the photographs still mix, especially around the eyes and brows."**
Also right, and it is where the regions overlap: a brow's box is grown by its
margin and reaches down over the socket, an eye's reaches up, so a closed eyelid
and a lowered brow were painted over the same skin in sequence — an average of
two photographs after all the work to stop being one. Each region is now cut
back by whatever the regions before it claimed, in build order: mouth, then
eyes, then brows. The eyes own the socket because that is the feature in it.

### Day 10, fifth pass — the profile was in the frames all along

**The owner was right and I was wrong about the cause.** Told for the third time
that the nose smears into dough at a turn, I answered that no photograph exists
past the ladder's end and a new clip was needed. They replied that the frames
exist — and named them. They do.

Measured over every dumped frame of the two turn clips:

| | frames tracked | yaw span | detail at the extremes |
|---|---|---|---|
| second turn clip | 71 of 80 | −54.5 … +31.5 | **43% at −54.5** |
| first turn clip | 68 of 80 | −25.0 … +63.1 | 1-3% past +34 |

So a sharp profile at −54.5° was sitting in the dump while the installed ladder
stopped at **−43**, and the engine was stretching a 32-degree photograph across
a 40-degree head.

**Why it was missed, which is the part worth keeping.** The ladder came from
`extract_avatar_frames.py`, which walks the *video* and rejects on filters that
are right for picking the best open mouth and wrong at a profile: a face
narrower than 380 px, identity ratios that "drifted" — they genuinely do change
when a head turns — and sharpness measured on the **near eye**, which is
foreshortened to almost nothing at 50 degrees. Every one of those throws away
exactly the frames a ladder most needs. Sampling was never the limit; the video
pass already sees every frame.

**`scripts/build_turn_ladder.py`** reads the dumped frames instead and asks one
question of each: what angle is this face at, and is it the sharpest thing at
that angle. One rung per band, no judgement across bands, and the one filter
kept is face size. It writes the rungs, retires the old ones, and rewrites the
manifest.

* **The ladder went from 23 rungs spanning −43…+32 to 40 rungs spanning
  −57…+62.** Median gap 2.8°, largest 7.9°.
* **Every rung is read back after it is written.** Cropping to the face is what
  makes a deep profile unreadable — the tracker loses a face it found easily in
  the whole frame — and the engine silently drops a source it cannot landmark.
  Five rungs are now written whole rather than cropped for that reason. Two
  still fail and are left out, at −58.6 and +55.0, either side of rungs that
  load.
* Frames re-dumped at **0.05 s**, 160 per clip, 960 in all, as the owner asked.

**What is still true about that side, stated precisely.** The rungs from +35 to
+62 come from the *first* turn clip, whose sweep was continuous, and they carry
1-4% of the sharpest rung's detail. They are correct in angle and soft in
texture. Rendered at 39.8° the torn fragments are gone and the face is
continuous — softer than frontal, not broken. Making that side sharp as well as
correct is what a new clip would buy, and only that.

**498 tests.**

### Day 10, sixth pass — a ladder for the smile, and why a soft rung can be worse than none

**Turning one way got worse, and it was the fix's own fault.** The rungs from
the first turn clip, at 1-4% of the sharpest rung's detail, were installed
wherever they filled a band — including at 35 and 38 degrees, two and six
degrees from a 95% rung at 32. Stretching a sharp photograph two degrees costs
nothing anyone can see; a smeared photograph costs everything. Soft rungs are
now kept **only where nothing sharp is within eight degrees**, which is where
stretching starts to tear. Two rungs dropped, the ladder holds 39.

**The smile, and this one is the owner's design.** Their words: *"we already
have the clip where he laughs — ten photographs from where the smile starts
through to the full smile."* They had diagnosed it exactly. A single smile
photograph cross-faded over a resting mouth fails twice at once: while it is
faint there are two mouths on screen, and at any weight the *shape* it draws is
the shape it was photographed at — so a small smile is a wide one at low
opacity. The lips never stretch; the teeth simply appear.

* `AssetRef.smile` joins `AssetRef.yaw`: an asset can now carry where it sits on
  the smile as well as at what angle. `scripts/build_smile_ladder.py` measures
  the dumped frames and files them along it.
* **The axis had to be built, not borrowed.** The smile blendshape reaches 1.0
  at the open smile and stays there through the laugh, so a ladder built on it
  stops exactly where the teeth begin. `smile x (1 + opening)` keeps rising:
  0.12 at a closed smile, 1.08 as the teeth appear, 1.75 at the laugh, and
  monotonic through the clip. A mouth open *without* a smile stays near zero, so
  speech still chooses its own shapes.
* Ten rungs installed, lip gap 0.002 to 0.085 — a smile forming, in
  photographs of it forming. The engine picks the nearest, one at a time, and
  `_eased` carries the change from one to the next over a third of a second.
  The two named smile photographs are retired: the ladder holds better versions
  of both.

**The brows, on the way past.** *"Two brows, and two more slightly above,
faint."* That is a photograph fading in over a portrait whose brows are
somewhere else. Every region has it while a photograph is faint; for a mouth the
difference is texture and survivable, for a brow it is *position*, which reads
as a second copy. The brows now keep the mesh for small raises and take the
photograph only past half commitment, ramped from there so there is no step.

**And a defect found while wiring the new ladder in:** a ladder holding only the
base portrait's own rung counted as a ladder, so an avatar with the four named
turn photographs and no ladder had them all suppressed and never turned at all.
Every avatar built since the ladder existed has had one, which is why it was
never seen.

**509 tests** (was 498).

### Day 10, seventh pass — the eyes get a ladder, and a blink gets its speed back

**The owner set the priority, and it is not the profile.** A screenshot at
roughly sixty degrees shows their own ear and the avatar's ear side by side —
real, and deliberately left alone: *"we need up to fifty degrees to be perfect.
What we need most is the eyes and the smile. Most of all!"*

**The eyes now have the smile's ladder.** `AssetRef.eyes` joins `yaw` and
`smile`, and one loader serves all three. The axis is the eyelid opening against
the widest in the clip, which is what the engine's own `relative_drop` computes
against its learned ceiling — so a rung is chosen by the number it was filed
under. Eight rungs from a resting eye to a shut one, built from the eyes clip
and the brows clip together. `source_eyes_closed` is retired: the ladder's top
rung is a better version of it, measured rather than named.

**And a defect the ladder exposed rather than caused.** Easing every region at
an eighth per frame is right for a smile and wrong for a lid: **a blink is
100-150 ms — three or four frames** — and at that rate the eye would still be
half shut by the time it was open again. Not a slow blink; a permanently
half-lidded avatar. The eyes ease at a third per frame, closing in three; the
rest of the face keeps the slower step, because nothing else on a face moves
like a lid.

**514 tests.** Three ladders now: 38 turn rungs, 11 smile rungs, 8 eye rungs.

### Day 10, eighth pass — a rung is shown whole, and easing measured in seconds

**"While the eye closes, the open one is still there over it, at low opacity."**
Right again, and the fade was the cause. The ladder fixed *which* photograph;
this fixes *how much of it*. For a named photograph the weight says how far
into that expression the frame is, and fading it in against the portrait is how
an expression arrives. **For a rung it says no such thing** — the amount is
already in which rung was chosen, so a three-tenths-shut eye mixed with a
wide-open portrait puts the wide-open one back underneath at exactly the
opacity being reported. A rung is now shown whole, at any amount. The choosing
has already answered the question the fade was trying to answer.

**Then 60 fps, which turned out to be two questions.** The first is a defect the
request exposed: the easing was measured **per frame**, so at 60 fps every
expression would have taken twice as long to arrive — the engine appearing to
get slower on a faster machine. It is measured in seconds now, from the
camera's own timestamps, and clamped so a dropped frame cannot let an
expression jump. A smile takes 0.27 s and a blink 0.1 s whatever the rate.

The second is whether 60 fps is reachable at all, and measured on the owner's
own driving clip, on this machine, it is not:

| stage | median |
|---|---|
| tracking | 8.1 ms |
| animate | 21.2 ms (p95 23.0) |
| **together** | **29.3 ms — a 34 fps ceiling before anything is drawn** |

60 fps needs the whole frame in 16.7 ms, and these two stages alone take nearly
twice that. The camera is a second wall: the ZV-E10 in USB streaming mode
delivers 720p30, so even a free engine would be duplicating frames. **The
smoothness left on the table is 20 to 30, not 30 to 60** — and where the missing
9 ms goes is already written down: the virtual camera with a consumer attached,
and the Qt preview converting a 720p frame in Python.

**520 tests.**

### Day 10, ninth pass — the lid axis gets its other half

**"Sometimes I do not widen my eyes at all and the screen does, as if I were
mad. It is a bit funny."** Measured on their own recording, the wide-eye
photograph fired on **4.6% of frames at a median weight of 0.11** — a sixth of a
full stare laid over a face doing nothing. The third time this exact shape has
appeared: the squint fired on 41% of frames and was dropped, the tilts on 99.2%
and were dropped, and now this.

But dropping is not the answer any more, because there is a better one. **The
lid ladder is now signed**: zero at rest, positive as the lids close, negative
when they open wider. Ten rungs from −0.38 to +0.56, and both named
photographs — `source_eyes_closed` and `source_eyes_wide` — retired into it. A
slight widening is now a photograph of a slight widening, shown whole.

**Rest is the median, not the widest**, and that correction is what makes the
axis mean anything. A clip containing an eyes-wide hold has its widest frame at
the wide end; measuring everything from there put a *resting* eye a third of the
way shut on its own scale, which is how the first eye ladder came out starting
at 0.10 instead of 0. Rest is where a face spends its time.

**And a rung is held until another is clearly nearer.** Because a rung is shown
whole, every change is a change of picture rather than of opacity — and the
eyelid measure moves a few percent frame to frame on a still face. Without the
hold the choice walked back and forth across a boundary several times a second.
With it, measured over 30 s of the owner's recording, the eye photograph changes
**1.8 times a second**, which is blinks and speech rather than noise.

Frame cost is unchanged by all of it: **animate 20.0 ms median, p95 22.4**,
against 21.2 before the ladders existed.

**527 tests.**

**Still on the list, and it is the same recipe:** the brows. They have the
commit floor from earlier today, which stops the faint double, but not a ladder
— and the material is already dumped, in the brows clip. `browInnerUp` against
`browDown` is a signed axis exactly like the lids.

### Day 10, tenth pass — the brows join them, and every family is now a ladder

The last one still driven by two named photographs, and the owner had reported
both halves of what that costs: *"a slight raise takes on a surprised face — the
brows do not actually go up"*, because the photograph shows a **full** raise
faded in by how far they moved; and *"two brows, and two more slightly above,
faint"*, because the photograph's brows are somewhere the portrait's are not.

`browInnerUp`/`browOuterUp` against `browDown` was always one signed number —
the engine computed it to weight the pair and then split it back into two
photographs. It is now the axis of a ladder of fifteen rungs from −1.00 to
+1.00, built from the brows clip alone. Both named photographs are retired.

**Where this leaves the library:**

| ladder | rungs | axis |
|---|---|---|
| turn | 38 | −57° to +62° of yaw |
| brows | 15 | −1 drawn down to +1 raised |
| eyes | 11 | −0.38 wide to +0.56 shut |
| smile | 11 | 0 resting to 1.75 laughing |

83 sources, and every expression family the engine has is now chosen by nearest
photograph rather than mixed by weight. The named roles that remain —
`mouth_o`, `mouth_pressed`, `mouth_wide`, `mouth_open`, `frown` — are the shapes
of speech, which are not a continuum and have no axis to be a ladder on.

Frame cost holds: **animate 20.4 ms median, p95 22.7**, against 21.2 before any
ladder existed. The brow photograph changes 0.9 times a second on the owner's
recording, the eye photograph 1.8.

**533 tests.**

### Day 10, eleventh pass — where the brows end and the eyes begin

A screenshot taken while opening the eyes: **two brows, a dark seam between
them, and an angry face for an instant.** The rest, in the owner's words, is

because that sits inside what the eyes had claimed. So the portrait's brow

|---|---|---|
| the eye | +0.91 | +76% |
| the nose bridge | +0.24 | +10% |
| the forehead | −0.22 | −4% |

## Day 11 — the sliver of iris, and the two candidates that were both wrong

**The defect the owner stopped the night on** — *"at a blink, a small piece of
the iris shows through the closed eyelid"* — is **fixed**, and it was neither of
the two things this file predicted the night before. Backlog 24 has the full
account; the short version is that measuring first was the whole difference.

**Both candidates were measured and ruled out.** The eye mask covers the closed
rung's own lid at **1.000**, worst pixel 0.996, and **1.000 at the outer corner**
where the sliver was — so the mask was never the cause. At full closure the
composite is 100% the rung with no portrait left underneath, so there is no
portrait iris to see. The containment *is* genuinely wrong — it holds the iris
inside the ring's bounding box rather than the ring, and 3 to 4 of every 5 iris
points still sit outside the lid, up to 4.7 px — but the pixels under those
triangles are eyelid either way, so it costs a one-pixel nick in the lash line.

**What it is: the arrival fade.** `_eased` ramps a region in over a tenth of a
second, and for the lids that rate was already ten a second — three frames. A
blink is three or four frames. So on every blink the eye spent its first two
frames as a **33% and 67% mix of a shut photograph over the wide-open portrait**,
and again on the way back up. That mix is a translucent iris under a closed lid,
up to **131 grey levels** from the frames either side. Over the recording,
**107 frames of 1847** were such a mix. It is now **0**.

Day 10's eighth pass removed exactly this fade from the *amount*. It survived in
the *arrival*, which is the same double exposure spread over three frames.

**And why it appeared on the 17th and not before: yesterday's own fix.** The two
shallow rungs, `eyes_c014` and `eyes_c017`, were refused at load for carrying
their brows inside the portrait's eye — correctly, the owner had named both. That
left the closing side running from a wide open eye at 0.0 to a **fully shut**
photograph at 0.32, which gave the fade the largest possible pair to average.

**So the second fix is in the builder.** A ladder builder takes the sharpest
frame per band and nothing else, so a band whose sharpest frame the engine will
refuse is lost *silently*. `refused_as_rung` is the load-time check, offered to
the builders; `build_eye_ladder` now takes the sharpest frame in each band **that
the engine will accept**, and says out loud what it passed over. Both holes are
filled from the same clip, at a cost in sharpness of 99 against 101 and 76
against 102.

| | before | after |
|---|---|---|
| frames that are a mix of two eye photographs | **107 of 1847** | **0** |
| eye rungs on the closing side | 4, none of them partly shut | 6 |
| `animate` mean / p95 | 21.20 / 23.93 ms | 20.91 / 23.91 ms |

**Two things measured and deliberately not done**, both in backlog 24: a hold at
the foot of the ladder, which more than doubles how long a closed eye is held
(median run 18 frames to 41) to buy eight fewer changes a minute; and containing
the iris by the lid outline, which removes the nick and puts a bright speck
where it was — one artefact for another.

**Still open, and it is material rather than code.** The clip has no genuinely

therefore a two-state cut. At 30 fps that is close to what a real lid does, but
a slow-closing clip would let it be a movement.

## Day 11, second pass — a shut eye's iris is tucked out of the way

**The owner looked again and it was still there, smaller**: *"the pupils, very
thin, low in height and long in width — a flat oval, for a very short time."*
That is a different defect from the cross-fade, and this time the night-before
note had named the right function for the wrong reason.

**Found by removal rather than by argument.** Rendering the same frames with
every triangle that touches an iris taken out of the mesh, the flat dark shape
goes and the lash line is continuous. That was the diagnosis; it was not, as it
turned out, the fix. Drawn on the frame, the iris ring sits
exactly on it, straddling the lash line, inside a lid ring that at full closure
crosses itself.

So: **containment shrinks the iris to fit the lid, and never puts it anywhere
else.**
Past a closure there is nothing left for an iris to be, and what the fan draws
instead is the lash line squeezed flat — which is a dark, wide, low oval, for
exactly as long as the lid is down.

**The line is measured, not chosen**, and it is written against this portrait's
own resting lid because that is the only kind of threshold this file has been
able to trust. The lid ring's height over its own width:

| the eye | height / width |
|---|---|
| base portrait | 0.323 |
| the four wide rungs | 0.403–0.448 |
| every frame with the eye open | 0.298–0.450 |
| every shut rung | 0.106–0.128 |
| every frame with the eye shut | 0.106–0.123 |

Nothing lies between 0.128 and 0.298, on either side of the warp, so the line
goes in the middle of the empty band: **three fifths of the portrait's own**.
Over the recording it never once misfires — 0 frames dropped with the lid
reading below 0.10, 0 kept above 0.30, and the closest an open frame came to the
line was 0.298 against a line at 0.194.

Each eye is decided on its own, because a wink is a face doing two different
things at once.

### And the first answer to it was wrong

**Taking the fan out of the mesh is what shipped first**, and the owner came
back with an arrow on each eye: *"the small thin blackness above the red arrows,
that is the problem."*

Those triangles were the only ones covering the inside of the lid. `warp_mesh`
lays a rigid placement of the whole composite down before the mesh paints, so
that no pixel is ever black — and with the fan gone that backdrop was all there
was inside a shut lid. It carries the photograph's own lash line, placed by one
similarity transform rather than by the mesh, so it lands a few pixels off:
**a second, thinner, darker lash line inside the shut lid.**

Painting the backdrop a flat colour and counting what stayed: **27 to 252 pixels
of every shut eye were painted by no triangle at all**, every one of them along
the lash line. That is the arrow, and it was mine.

**So the ring is tucked instead of removed.** A shut eye's iris ring is pulled
in to 0.35 of its distance from the lid ring's own centroid. The fan stays, the
hole never opens, and what those triangles paint is the lid's own pixels.
Scaling about the lid's centroid is what makes the two meshes agree — it is a
linear function of the ring, so the fan comes out near an identity — and the
triangles between the tucked ring and the lid *grow* rather than shrink, which
is what the earlier attempt at clipping the iris to the lid outline got wrong:
it drove them to no area, and an affine fitted to three nearly-collinear points
put a **bright** speck where the dark one had been.

| | containment only | fan dropped | ring tucked |
|---|---|---|---|
| the flat dark oval | present | gone | gone |
| the thin line inside the lid | — | **present** | gone |
| pixels painted by no triangle | 0 | 27–252 | **0** |
| `animate` mean | 21.20 ms | 21.48–21.51 ms | 21.79–21.91 ms |
| p95 | 23.93 ms | 24.58–24.60 ms | 24.97–25.15 ms |

Three runs each, same machine, same recording. The tuck costs **0.3 ms of mean
and 0.5 of p95** against dropping the fan, because the forty triangles it gives
back are drawn again. The budget is 33.3 ms and the frame is at 22.

**Correcting the first pass's own number.** It reported `animate` at 34 ms and
called it the largest open item in the project. It was not the engine — it was
this machine, running six other measurements while the figure was taken.
Re-measured on a quiet one it is the 20.4 / 22.5 the budget section already
records. **No frame-rate problem was found**, and the 34 ms line has been struck
from the list below. Two wrong numbers in one day, both from measuring in
passing, is the argument for item 1 below.

**565 tests.**

## Day 12 — the quality measure becomes a tool, and the first thing it measured was the frame cost

**`scripts/measure_quality.py`.** One clip, fixed frames, one report, and every
number printed with the conditions that make it mean something. It was the first
item on the code list for a stated reason: twice on day 11 a figure taken in
passing reached a commit message and had to be corrected the next day.

It renders `driving.mkv` through the tracker and the engine exactly as the
application does — from frame 0 every time, because both are stateful — and
measures four things per frame: the **warp residual** (the pixels left after a
rigid fit of the blended mesh onto the tracked face, which is how much the
picture is being stretched), the **near-eye sharpness** of the rendered frame,
the whole-**face sharpness**, and the cost of `animate` alone. Each is reported
for all frames, frontal frames and turned frames separately, because the turn
fix on day 8 was nearly invisible in a single average.

It does **not** combine them into a score. A residual in pixels and a Laplacian
variance do not add up to anything, and a weighted total would look like a
measurement while being an opinion.

**What it refuses.** `--baseline` compares two runs and stops dead if they did
not measure the same thing: a different clip, a different frame range, or a
different photograph library — the last because day 9 rebuilt every ladder
without touching a line of engine code, and every number moved. The frame cost
is withheld separately from the geometry, since a busy machine does not move a
pixel: repeated runs of the same clip produced **identical residuals to the last
decimal**, which is the proof that half this report is exact.

### The baseline, 1817 frames of `driving.mkv`

Machine rested first, and every number below reproduced exactly across four
runs taken in three different machine states.

|---|---|---|---|
| warp residual, mean px | 7.59 | 7.60 | 7.07 |
| warp residual, p50 / p95 | 6.91 / 12.24 | 6.91 / 12.26 | 6.70 / 8.28 |
| near-eye sharpness | 215 | 213 | 337 |
| face sharpness | 140 | 140 | 148 |
| `animate`, mean / p50 / p95 ms | 21.44 / 21.71 / 24.48 | 21.49 / 21.76 / 24.49 | 19.28 / 19.29 / 21.24 |
| frames | 1817 | 1783 | **34** |

**Thirty-four frames.** The recorded clip holds a turn past ten degrees on
**1.9%** of its length, so the largest open item on the list is being judged on
two thirds of a second of video. That is not a shortcoming of the clip — it was

### The frame cost, and the trap the tool walked into first

**The same 301 frames, the same commit, measured four times, read 21.5 ms once
and 34.8 to 35.8 ms three times.** Nothing about the engine changed between
them. What changed was how long this machine had been working.

| the machine | probe, start → end | `animate` over 301 frames |
|---|---|---|
| rested five minutes | 0.384 → 0.408 ms (+6%) | **21.53** |
| after half an hour of runs | 0.388 → 0.626 ms (+61%) | 35.66, 35.75, 34.76 |

The probe is a fixed OpenCV warp timed before and after every run — the tool's
own reading of how fast the machine is going. Measured directly as well, outside
the tool: probe, sixty seconds of many-threaded warping, probe again — **0.392 →
0.572 ms, and still 0.601 after twenty seconds of rest.** Minutes, not seconds,
are what this machine needs to come back.

**21.53 ms on a rested machine is the 21.4 ms this document already carried**,
which is the reassuring half of the finding. The other half is that the same
measurement taken half an hour into a working session reads **65% higher**, and
nothing in a printed number says which of the two it is. Every frame-cost figure
in this file was taken by a scratch script that recorded neither.

That is the whole argument for the tool, made by the tool, on its first day —
and the first version of it got the interpretation wrong in the other direction:
it treated the slowdown as another program competing and refused to report the
frame cost at all, which refused every full-clip run. Both readings are now
recorded, and a comparison is made **start against start**.

**And then the same discipline had to be applied to the finding itself.** The
first draft of this section said the engine costs 35 ms in the state a real call
runs it in, and offered that as the explanation for item 6 — *stages measured
29 ms, the owner sees 20*. It is not. A **full 1847-frame run on a rested
machine warms it by 7%** and costs **21.44 ms**: first tenth 22.17, last tenth
21.27, no drift worth the name. One clip does not heat this machine. Half an
hour of clips back to back does.

So item 6 stays exactly where it was. What the day produced instead is a
narrower and more useful fact: **`animate` costs 21.4 ms, confirmed on a rested
machine, and any figure taken later in a working session will read up to 65%
higher for reasons that have nothing to do with the code.** Whether a
thirty-minute call puts the machine in that state is **scenario S5 of the
performance plan**, which has still never been run.

## Day 12, second pass - the ladder was built out of the tracker's own lag

The owner generated the slow-close clip, and checking it found something larger
than the clip. **`build_eye_ladder.py` was not measuring eyelids.**

Every ladder builder handed each still to one tracker with
`capture_timestamp=0.0`. The tracker runs in `RunningMode.VIDEO` and carries

### Smart App Control turned on and killed the tracker

## Day 13, second pass — the refiner exists, runs on CUDA in 1.3 ms, and is short of data

**CUDA had never been used by anything.** Before today it appeared in exactly
two places in the repository: a `device: "auto"|"cuda"|"cpu"` field nothing
reads, and `scripts/check_gpu.py` from day 1. The engine is entirely CPU — a
~900-triangle mesh warp whose cost is per-call overhead rather than pixels — so
the 5070 Ti has been idle for twelve days.

**And it should stay idle for the warp.** Moving 900 small `warpAffine` calls
onto the GPU makes them slower, not faster, and `cv2.cuda` is not in the pip
wheel at all (`getCudaEnabledDeviceCount()` is 0, `build has CUDA: False`), so
using it means building OpenCV from source and shipping a CUDA runtime to the
customer. The GPU's place is the refiner, which is where the plan always put it.

### `scripts/make_training_pairs.py` — the week-1 item that did not exist

**The problem it solves is that there is no ground truth.** The avatar is a
generated person, so for any live frame the right answer has never been
photographed and never will be.

It exists inside one clip. Every clip in `avatars/*/frames` is that same face
moving, so frame **A** warped onto frame **B**'s mesh has an answer: **B
itself**. The input carries the artefacts and the target carries none of them,
and nothing in the pair is invented by us.

The warp is the shipped `warp_mesh` with the same triangulation and the same
face box, so what the refiner learns to undo is what the engine will hand it.
The mask travels with each pair, because outside the face the two images differ
by the whole background and a refiner scored on that would spend itself there.

**1000 pairs at 256×256, 289 MiB, from seven clips.** What is in them:

| | mean of the face | worst 1% of it |
|---|---|---|
| p5 | 1.9 | 11.0 |
| p50 | 6.3 | 41.0 |
| p95 | 15.2 | 87.7 |

The worst-1% column is the one that matters and is why it is reported at all:
a sheared triangle by an eye corner is a handful of pixels wrong by a lot, and
it moves the mean by almost nothing. **Every complaint the owner has made is a
worst-1% complaint.** By frame distance the difficulty is a clean curriculum —
median worst-1% runs 26 at ±5 frames to 70 at ±45.

### `scripts/train_refiner.py`, and what it actually achieved

345k parameters: two stride-2 convolutions down, four residual blocks, two
nearest-neighbour upsamples back, and a **zero-initialised delta** so an
untrained refiner is exactly the identity and cannot start worse than the warp.
The split holds out a **whole clip** — frames five apart are near-duplicates,
so a random split reports a number that means nothing.

| | mean | worst 1% |
|---|---|---|
| the warp alone, held out | 9.11 | 60.33 |
| the refiner, best epoch | **8.76** | **55.46** |

**−3.8% on the mean and −8% on the worst 1%.** Real, measured on a clip it never
saw, and small.

**Inference: 1.30 ms median, 2.54 ms p95, on one 256×256 tile.** B001 predicted
1.83 ms fp32 for this shape on day 1 and it came in under that. Against
`animate`'s 21.4 ms and a 33.3 ms budget, **the refiner fits with room to
spare** — and that was the single largest unknown in the four-week plan.

### Why it is small, measured rather than guessed

Training loss fell from 6.79 to 4.86 while held-out error stopped improving at
about epoch 30 and then drifted back. That is overfitting, and the fix it
implies was checked rather than assumed:

| training pairs | held-out mean | gain over the warp |
|---|---|---|
| 214 | 8.96 | −0.15 |
| 427 | 8.83 | −0.28 |
| 853 | 8.76 | −0.35 |

**The curve is still climbing.** More data is the answer, and it is now a
measurement instead of an instinct. The cheapest 5× is on disk already — the
set was built at `--stride 5`, and stride 1 gives about 9000 pairs from the same

**One thing to settle before spending a night on 9000 pairs.** Part of the 9.11

recover what A does not contain — a closed lid, teeth behind a mouth that was

against 9.11" has no denominator. Measured next, below.

## Day 13, third pass — the denominator, and it is smaller than hoped

`scripts/measure_refiner_ceiling.py`. Three numbers on the same frames, all on
`video-10`, which is the clip the refiner was held out on:

| | mean | worst 1% |
|---|---|---|
| the warp machinery alone (self-warp) | 0.00 | 0.00 |
| **one source — what ships** | **9.06** | **58.77** |
| one source, flat exposure removed | 9.12 | 58.46 |
| **the consensus of every source** | **7.05** | **47.14** |

*Self-warp* is frame B warped onto B's **own** mesh. It comes out exactly zero,
which is itself worth recording: **when the two meshes agree the warp is
lossless.** Every pixel of error the engine makes comes from the geometry
differing, none of it from resampling or triangle edges.

*Consensus* is every available source warped onto B and the per-pixel median
taken. Where independent warps agree, the content was in all of them; where they
disagree, some are artefacts and the median discards them. A refiner reads
**one** warped image, so it has strictly less to work with than that median —
which makes the consensus an **optimistic** estimate of the best any
single-input refiner could do.

**Over all seven clips** (80 targets) the same measurement reads 7.37 / 4.77 and
a headroom of **35%**. `video-10` is a harder clip than the average, which is
why it is quoted separately: it is where the refiner's own numbers were taken,
and mixing the two would compare a share against the wrong whole.

### What that makes the first refiner worth

| | available | the refiner took | share |
|---|---|---|---|
| mean | 2.01 | 0.35 | **17%** |
| worst 1% | 11.62 | 4.87 | **42%** |

**The refiner is not underperforming — it is already taking 42% of everything
available on the metric that matches what the owner sees.** And the prize is
bounded: **the whole of the refiner's possible contribution is 22% of the error
the engine makes today.** The other 78% is not reachable by any network reading
one warped frame.

### Two explanations for the floor, both tested and both wrong

Neither was assumed away, and both would have changed what to do next:

*   **Not the mask's edge.** The edge runs through hair, ears and the jaw's
    silhouette, where two frames of one face differ by a strand. Split, the
    consensus error is **7.22 on the face's core against 6.43 on the ring** —
    the floor is *on the face*, not at its border.
*   **Not a flat exposure difference.** Shifting each channel's mean onto the
    target's before scoring moves the error from 9.06 to **9.12**, so a global
    tone shift explains none of it. This one mattered: the pairs stop at
    ``warp_mesh`` and never run ``_match_colour``, so had the answer been yes,
    every number above would have been measuring a correction the engine already
    applies.

The floor is real, spread across the face, and it is what a 2-D landmark warp
cannot do: skin shading that moves with the flesh, speculars, hair, and the
parts of a 3-D rotation no triangle can express.

**One caveat on the ceiling, stated because the whole point of the number is to
be trusted.** The consensus bound assumes a network reading one image cannot
beat a median of five. That is reasonable and it is not a theorem — a trained
network carries priors about faces that a median does not, and could in
principle exceed it. Read 22% as an estimate with the error bar on the
optimistic side.

### What it changes

*   **The refiner is worth finishing, not worth a fortnight.** The remaining
    prize is about 1.7 of mean and 6.8 of worst-1%. More data is still the
    lever — the learning curve was still climbing — but the target is now known.
*   **The larger prize is not in the network.** 78% of the error is the source
    frame being the wrong picture, which is the *ladder's* job, not the
    refiner's. Every clip the owner generates moves that number; no amount of
    training does.

## Day 13, fourth pass — the brow zero was the first frame, for a minute

Day 13's first pass left this named and undiagnosed: `_brow_amount` had a median
of **−0.202** over `driving.mkv`, so a person talking normally, brows where they
keep them, read a fifth of the way into a frown.

**It is `track_level`, and it is the seed.** That method was a plain slow mean
seeded from frame 0, with none of the acceleration `relative_pose` and
`relative_rise` both have. Measured: **frame 0 of `driving.mkv` reads a brow
height of 0.1489 against the clip's median of 0.1014** — 47% high — and at
`_POSE_RATE` that takes a seventeen-second time constant to walk off. Tenth by
tenth the amount came out

```
-0.455  -0.378  -0.276  -0.201  -0.119  -0.279  -0.226  -0.175  +0.100 ...
```

decaying across the whole recording. **The bias was the first frame, still being
paid for a minute later.**

**The other candidate was checked and dismissed.** The brow signal is genuinely
skewed — brows raise much further than they lower, so a *mean* sits above a
*median* by construction. Measured, that skew is **1.7%**, a fortieth of what
was seen. It is the seed, not the skew.

**Scope, checked rather than assumed.** `track_level` has exactly one caller:
`_brow_amount`, under the name `browHeight`. The eyes use `relative_rise` /
`relative_drop` and the mouth uses `relative_expression`, both of which already
warm up. The worry that this reached three families was wrong.

The fix is the remedy its two neighbours already carry: for the first
`_LEVEL_WARMUP_FRAMES` readings the rate floors at a `_LEVEL_WINDOW_FRAMES`

## Day 13, fifth pass — the iris was a diamond, and is now an octagon

The owner, testing: *"when I look slightly to the side with my eyes the iris
changes shape and stops being a circle — it gets partly pointed. The rest is
normal."* And separately: *"sometimes a greenish-bluish colour becomes part of
the iris."*

**Two reports, one mechanism, and it is geometry.** MediaPipe gives a centre and
**four** boundary points per iris, so the mesh's iris is a **diamond inscribed
in the iris circle** — and an inscribed square covers only **2/π = 63.7%** of a
circle's area. The other **36%** is four circular segments belonging to no iris
triangle at all: they are carried by the fan running out to the eyelid.
`_place_irises` then moves the *target's* iris by the gaze while the source's
stays where the portrait's is, so that fan is sheared. The compressed side folds
iris pixels into a corner; the stretched side pulls sclera in under the iris
outline. **A point, and a colour that is not iris.**

### And the limits were not where the comment said

Measured on 1847 frames, sideways gaze as a fraction of eye width:

| | |
|---|---|
| median | **0.1172** |
| p95 | 0.2196 |
| max | 0.2633 |
| where `_GAZE_LIMIT_X / _GAZE_SCALE` clamps | **0.1182** |

The comment said *"the scale keeps normal gaze comfortably inside them"*. **The
owner's ordinary gaze sits exactly on the clamp**: the iris is at or past the
limit on about half of a call. The limits are unchanged anyway — the defect they
guard against has been attacked from the other side instead, and whether a finer
iris earns a wider limit is a question for a measurement.

### The fix

Four more points per iris, on the iris's own outline, between the four the
tracker gives. A regular octagon covers **90.0%** of the circle, so the share
the shearing fan is responsible for falls from **36% to 10% — 3.6-fold**.

They are computed rather than tracked, in `_pixel_points`, which is the one
place a mesh is built — so the portrait and the live face get them by the same
rule, which is the only property a warp requires. Each takes the radius of the
two neighbours it sits between, so an iris the tracker reports as slightly oval
keeps its shape; and they are bisected by angle rather than by landmark number,
so which index is "top" is not something this file has to know.

### Measured

| | before | after |
|---|---|---|
| **near-eye sharpness, all** | 336 | **359** |
| **near-eye sharpness, turned** | 447 | **483** |
| face sharpness | 168 | 171 |
| warp residual, all | 7.07 px | 7.30 |
| mesh | 139 points, 229 triangles | **147, 245** |

**Near-eye sharpness is the metric for exactly this region and it moved +23**,
and +36 on turned frames. Rendered side by side against the camera on frames
390–392 — the owner's furthest sideways glance — the polygonal outline and the
dark corner notches are gone.

**The residual rose and the explanation is not verified.** The likely reason is
that it is an average over the mesh points, and the mesh gained eight points
which are *deliberately* displaced by gaze rather than placed by the fit — so
the iris now carries twice the weight in an average it was always going to
score badly in. That is a change in what the number is over rather than in what
the picture looks like, but it is a candidate and not a measurement.

**And the frame cost is unknown.** 245 triangles against 229 is **+7.0%**, and
`warp_mesh` costs one OpenCV call per triangle, so the honest expectation is
about +1.5 ms on `animate`'s 21.4 ms. The comparison refused to report it —
one run was on a machine 3% warm and the other 52% — so it needs a rested
measurement before it is believed.

**620 tests.** Two fixtures had to stop hard-coding the mesh's shape: one built
a mesh by hand instead of through `_pixel_points`, and one wrote a fixed list of
five iris points into a list that is now nine. The overlap test is eroded by the
fence's width, because the octagon's vertices sit on the iris outline where the
diamond's edges stopped short of it — six pixels of the hull fell below the bar
and every one was on its boundary.

### 2. The iris still takes a colour that is not iris

**Cause, and the code already names the risk for the other axis:**

where the lid outline narrows; the fan triangles there are compressed to almost
no area, and an affine fitted to three nearly-collinear points is unstable.

outline with a margin, so the fan can never collapse, rather than trusting a
fixed `_GAZE_LIMIT_X`.

is separately impossible today: `animate` is 21.4 ms against a 16.7 ms budget.)

## Day 14 — speech is one-dimensional, the material is on disk, and the driving clip is mislabelled

`scripts/measure_speech_plane.py`, the cheap first step day 13 asked for: *"measure
whether the existing `speech` clip already covers the width × parting plane. If it
does, the material is on disk and this is a builder. If it does not, it is a clip."*

It does. And two other things fell out of the same pass, one of which is larger
than the question that was asked.

### The plane is the engine's own, and so are its units

Both axes are what `engine_warp` already computes, normalised by the spans it
normalises them with, so a unit here is a unit there:

*   **widened** — `_smiling`, the mouth's width against the outer eye corners,
    above rest, over `_SMILE_WIDEN_SPAN`. The driver's rest is the floor
    `_Neutral` learns; a source's rest is the portrait's own mouth.
*   **parted** — `_lip_parting`, the inner lips' gap on the same denominator,
    less `_SMILE_LIP_CLOSED`, over `_SMILE_OPEN_SPAN`.

### The open design question, answered: one ladder, not a grid

Measured over the 240 frames of `driving.mkv` where the owner is actually
speaking:

| | p5 | p50 | p95 | max |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| widened | 0.000 | 0.046 | 0.118 | 0.133 |
| **parted** | 0.000 | **0.202** | **0.947** | **1.212** |

They move together at **r = −0.049**, and one direction carries **98.4%** of the
demand: **(−0.006 widened, +1.000 parted)**. Speech parts the lips and barely
widens the mouth. *"An eye is one-dimensional while speech is two"* was the
worry; on this face, in the engine's own units, it is not. **A ladder on lip
parting, and no grid.**

### And it names the mechanism behind *"it does not follow me"*

The mouth is chosen by `widened × (1 + parted)` — `_smile_amount`. During speech
`widened` sits at 0.046 and hardly moves, so the scalar hardly moves either:

*   the demand is **0.262** wide about its own centre;
*   frames the scalar cannot tell apart still sit **0.214** apart;
*   so the scalar removes **18%** of the spread and keeps 82% of it hidden.

That is the whole complaint in one number. The ladder holds still while the lips
open and close over a full span, and everything the driver does is then carried
by the mesh stretching a closed mouth — which is exactly what day 13 photographed.

### The material is on disk, so this is a builder

Nearest photograph to each speech frame, on the plane:

| | sources | median | p90 | worst |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| the installed library | 16 | 0.047 | 0.221 | **0.567** |
| **the `speech` clip** | **240** | **0.015** | **0.034** | **0.066** |
| `video-10`, the mouth-open clip | 160 | 0.017 | 0.206 | 0.632 |

**The speech clip's worst frame is better than the installed library's p90.** All
240 of its frames pass `|yaw| ≤ 12` and `face ≥ 380 px`, its identity drift
against the portrait is 0.032 median and 0.095 worst against a tolerance of
0.08, and at a tenth of the axis per rung there are **no holes**: every band the
driver visits, the clip can fill, and it goes on to 2.3 where the driver stops
at 1.2.

**One caveat, and it decides where each rung comes from.** The speech clip is
720p with a **389 px** face; `video-10` is 4K with **1009 px**, against a
portrait of 455. Band winners' sharpness reads 337/380/286/203 from `video-10`
and 99/87/66/70 from the speech clip. `video-10` reaches only to parted 0.6 and
covers 79% of speech frames; above that the 720p clip is the only material there
is. **So: build from both** — the builder takes the sharpest frame per band and
will pick the 4K clip below 0.6 by itself — and put a 1080p 9:16 re-shoot of the
speech clip on the list beside the turn clips.

### The driving clip's segment map does not describe the driving clip

Found while looking for the speech frames, and it is the larger finding.

`recordings/driving.json` names which frames were recorded against which
instruction. **The content lags the labels, and the lag grows to about 450
frames — fifteen seconds — by the end.** Verified in the pictures, not inferred:

| frame | labelled | actually |
|---|---|---|
| 900 | *"Smile widely, showing your teeth"* | eyes shut, mouth closed |
| 1050 | *"Open your mouth wide"* | a closed-mouth smile |
| 1200 | *"TALK"* | a wide smile |
| 1500 | *"Turn your head slowly LEFT"* | mouth wide open |
| 1560–1800 | *"Turn RIGHT" / "Raise your eyebrows" / "SIT STILL again"* | **the speech** |

Measured the same way: the eight seconds labelled `TALK` contain **two** mouth
openings in 8 s — 0.25 a second, against the three to five a second of ordinary
speech — and the segment labelled *"Open your mouth wide, then close it"* has
none at all and a p95 lip parting of 0.0116.

The recorder is not at fault: `record_driving_clip.py` stamps each boundary from
the frames it has actually written, and every segment in the sidecar comes out
at 29.3–30.0 fps. **The person lags the prompts, and the lag accumulates**, which
no amount of correct frame counting can see.

**Three consequences.**

*   **`measure_quality.py --segment` selects the wrong frames.** One saved report
    used it — `day12-closed-segment.json`, *"the two seconds of held-shut eyes"*,
    over frames 654–743, where the eyes are open. The closure is around 820–910.
    The frontal/turned split is **safe**: `_by_angle` reads the measured yaw, not
    the sidecar.
*   **`driving.mkv` contains no completed head turn.** `|yaw|` stays under 4.6°
    until frame 1800, then rises to 39.8° at the last frame — the final
    instructions ran off the end of the recording. Every quality report since day
    12 shows **34 turned frames out of 1817**, 1.9%, one direction, cut off. Every
    "turned" number this project has published rests on those 34 frames.
*   **The quality measure needs a new driving clip**, recorded with the prompts
    held until the movement is done rather than on a timer, and checked against
    its own map before it is used.

### And an engine finding, on the way

`_Neutral.relative_expression("smileAmount", …)` is **advanced twice per frame**:
once by `_smile_share` inside `_source_weights`, and again by `_smile_amount`
when it chooses the rung. Both learn from the reading, so the smile floor chases
the mouth at double the intended rate — `_FLOOR_FALL` 0.15 becomes 0.2775 — and
`_smile_amount`, reading second, sees a systematically smaller widening than
`_smile_share` did. It picks a rung slightly too low.

This is the exact hazard `_Neutral.floor_of` was written to prevent, and the head
pose is careful about it one screen further down: *"Read, not learn:
`_source_weights` has already advanced the resting angle for this frame."* The
smile floor is not. **Not fixed today** — it is measured, named, and it belongs
with the speech ladder, because that is the code that will read it.

## Day 14, second pass — the speech ladder, and one photograph replaced by twelve

Built, installed, and measured against the frames it was built for.
`scripts/build_speech_ladder.py`, and the engine gained a fourth axis.

### What it is

**Fourteen rungs, one per tenth of the parting axis, from two clips.** The
sharpest frame in each band wins, which sorted the two sources by itself: the 4K
`video-10` took the bottom three rungs at sharpness 337, 329 and 88, and the
720p speech clip took the eleven above them — which is the part of the range
that had no photograph at all before. No holes between the lowest rung and the
highest.

**The axis is absolute, and it is the only one that is.** Where a mouth *rests*
is a fact about the person — the owner's sits at 0.53 of an eye span wide — but
where lips *touch* is not: they touch at zero on every face. So `_speech_amount`
has nothing to learn and nothing to warm up, which is worth having on the one
axis that moves several times a second.

**Two ladders share the mouth, and what separates them is which way the mouth is
moving.** The engine already drew that line for the named photographs —
`source_smile` takes `opening x smiling`, `source_mouth_open` takes
`opening x (1 - smiling)` — and the same line is drawn between the two ladders,
on the same quantity. Measured on the owner: `_smile_share` sits near 0.12 while
he speaks and reaches 0.80 at a broad smile. `_one_per_region` keeps whichever
scores higher and shows it whole. **No new threshold was added.**

That needed one new thing: `_nearest_rung` can now be given a weight separately
from the amount. Everywhere else the amount answers both questions — the eye
ladder's 0.3 rung *is* a photograph of an eye three tenths shut — but scaling the
amount here would have chosen a rung at the wrong parting.

## Day 14, third pass — the owner tested it, and three separate faults came out

He ran it within the hour and reported, with two screenshots: *"it follows the
mouth movements badly"*, *"the difference between these two photos is the beard
colour"*, *"the lips are also a little thicker when you open your mouth
slightly"*, and — decisively — *"the beard colour differs and that makes a big
difference."*

He was right on all of it, and the three faults underneath turned out to be
independent. Two were mine from the second pass; the third has been in every
ladder this project has built.

### One: every multi-folder ladder was built with the clock running backwards

**The largest of the three, and it is not confined to the mouth.**

`timestamp_of` reads the millisecond out of the dumped frame's file name, which
starts again at zero for each folder. The builders hand every folder to *one*
tracker, so from the second folder onward MediaPipe is given timestamps earlier
than the ones before — and MediaPipe in VIDEO mode reasons about how far a face
can have moved from the gap between timestamps.

Measured, on the same file, in the same run:

| `video-10/0057_02850ms.jpg` | lip parting |
|---|---|
| measured after the speech folder | **0.35** |
| measured on its own | **0.51** |

Two thirds of a band, on a frame that then wins one. The effect on the ladder
was not subtle: with a fresh tracker per folder, `video-10` yields **six 4K
rungs reaching a parting of 0.56**, where before it appeared to stop at 0.38 and
gave three.

This is the second half of a defect whose first half was fixed on 2026-08-21 —
`timestamp_of` exists because every builder used to pass a *constant* timestamp,
and the eye ladder was built out of the tracker's own lag. The constant is gone;
the reset was not seen.

**The fix is one line in each of five builders, and it is applied to all five.**
The eye and smile ladders that are installed today were both built from two
folders and carry whatever this cost them. **Rebuilding them is not done here**
— each needs its own before-and-after, and doing it in the same pass as this
one would mix three changes into one number.

### Two: the ladder was picking 720p frames over 4K ones

`best_per_band` ranked candidates by Laplacian variance alone, the way the other
four builders do. That is right when every candidate comes from clips of one
resolution and wrong the moment they do not: **a sharpness is a property of the
pixels a face was drawn with**, so a 389 px face and a 1009 px face cannot be
compared by it.

Ranking that way put eleven 720p rungs into a library whose every other source
is 4K. Both of the owner's visual complaints are that one cause: a 389 px face
scaled up to the portrait's 455 is soft, its beard smears into the skin — *"the
beard colour differs"* — and the generator drew that face with fuller lips —
*"the lips are a little thicker."*

Now the widest face in a band wins, and sharpness only breaks ties within a
tenth of it. Combined with the clock fix:

| | first ladder | now |
|---|---|---|
| rungs from 4K material | 3 | **6** |

### Three: the tone match was fitted on the wrong pixels, and clamped

Two faults in one function, both measured.

**The clamp.** `_SKIN_SHIFT_LIMIT` was a single 12 of 255, set when every source

darker than the portrait. Measured across all 95 sources, the *spread* between

colour shift, they are exposures. So the limit is now split: a correction whose
channels agree gets `_SKIN_SHIFT_FLAT_LIMIT`, 60; one whose channels disagree
keeps 12, because that is a different light rather than a different exposure.

**The sample.** A whole-face median is the right sample for a turn rung, which
*is* the whole head, and the wrong one for a region: a face is not lit evenly.
Applied in full it left the mouth region 12 to 17 of 255 too *bright*, where
clamping it had left the same region 20 to 26 too dark.

region, outside the portrait's own feature and outside that photograph's. Skin
in both pictures by construction, so no cavity, no teeth and no shut lid can
reach the estimate. **Per photograph, and that is not a detail**: one ring
avoiding every rung's open mouth at once came to *five pixels* on this avatar.

## Day 14, fourth pass — speech is two-dimensional after all, and the clamp is why I said it was not

The owner sent two frames: *"in the first photo I am saying O, in the second

### The morning's measurement was taken through a clamp

It said speech is one-dimensional: over the frames of `driving.mkv` where he
speaks, the widening carried **1.6%** of the demand and the parting 98.4%, at
r = -0.049. A ladder, not a grid.

**That was measured on `_widened`, which is floored at zero.** Every mouth
*narrower* than rest reads as exactly the same value as a resting one — and a
narrower mouth is precisely what an "oo" and an "o" are. Measured again with the
floor taken off:

| | narrower than the portrait's rest | width within one band of parting |
|---|---|---|
| the speech clip | **20% of frames** | runs 0.25 narrower to 0.22 wider |
| the driver, speaking | **50% of frames** | 0.03 to 0.23 wide |

So half of the driver's speech and a fifth of the clip's sat at the same value,
and the axis could not see any of it. **A rung chosen by parting alone therefore
had an essentially arbitrary width** — anything within a span of 0.42 — which is
an O rendered as an A, exactly as reported.

**The tool is what was wrong, not the reasoning applied to it.**
`measure_speech_plane.py` placed both the demand and the supply through the
engine's own clamped quantity, because using the engine's own arithmetic is the
rule this project works by. Here that rule hid the answer: the engine could not
see the second axis either, which is the defect rather than the measurement's
excuse for missing it.

### So: a grid, on two axes

`speech_width` joins `speech` on the asset, both in the engine's own normalised
units, so a unit of width and a unit of parting are the same size and the
nearest rung is a plain Euclidean distance on the plane. `_nearest_speech`
replaces `_nearest_rung` for this family alone.

**32 rungs**, one per 0.10 of parting by 0.08 of width, from `video-10`,
`video-09` and the speech clip, with the bigger face still winning a cell and
sharpness only breaking ties. Cells holding a single frame are dropped: one
frame is a moment of the clip rather than a shape the mouth makes.

### And the transitions, which he was also right about

Measured over his eight seconds of speech, on the one-axis ladder:

| | one axis | the grid |
|---|---|---|

| held, at the median | 2 frames, 67 ms | **6 frames, 183 ms** |
| runs lasting a single frame | **28 of 62** | **4 of 32** |

A photograph held for one frame is not a mouth moving, it is a mouth flickering.

| 0.00 | 12.5 | 1 fr | 57 | 0.11 |
| 0.05 | 9.4 | 2 fr | 30 | 0.15 |

## Day 15 — the lip parting carried the frame's shape, and it was already installed

**The day was planned around one generation.** The owner produced the speech
clip at 1080p 9:16, longer material for the thin top of the grid. Measuring it
found the defect below instead, and the clip is now the least interesting thing

### What the clip measured

`Generated video 1 (13).mp4`, 1080x1920, 8.0 s, 192 frames. Through
`extract_avatar_frames.py`: **128 usable**, 56 motion blurred, 8 identity
drifted, **face width median 491 px** against the portrait's 455 and the old
720p speech clip's 389. Frames measure 200-900 of Laplacian variance where the
old clip's measure 50-100.

**And on the engine's own axis it read as a mouth that barely opens** — max 0.64
where the old 720p take of the same sentence reaches 2.30. That is what sent the
morning into the measure rather than into the grid.

### The defect

:func:`_lip_parting` divided the inner lips' gap by the **eye span**. The
landmarks are normalised over the frame, so a vertical distance over a
horizontal one is multiplied by the frame's aspect ratio. Proved both
directions, sequentially, with a fresh tracker each run:

| | as generated | letterboxed to the other shape |
|---|---|---|
| the new clip, 9:16 | parted p50 0.13, max **0.64** | to 16:9: p50 0.54, max **2.14** |
| the old clip, 16:9 | parted p50 0.65, max **2.30** | to 9:16: p50 0.16, max **0.70** |

The same frames. The aspect-free reading — the gap over face height — does not
move at all: 0.032/0.111 for the new clip and 0.038/0.120 for the old, either
way round. **The two clips photograph nearly the same mouth**, and one of them
was being read at a third of it.

**This file already documents this bug three times.** :func:`_eye_opening` says
it in full — *"a height divided by a width carries the frame's aspect ratio as a
factor"* — and the lids, the brows and `_lip_gap` were each fixed by dividing by
face height instead. `_lip_parting` was written after them and reached for the
eye span because it is the steadier anchor across a re-detection. It is not
worth the frame's shape. There was no test; there is now, beside the brows'.

### It was not waiting for a 9:16 clip. It was already installed

**Every 4K clip in this library is 2160x3840.** `video-05` through `video-10`,
all of them portrait. Only the old 720p speech clip is 16:9, and the camera is
16:9.

`video-10`'s frames filed at a third of their real opening in the low bands, and
the 720p clip's filed correctly in the high ones. A mouth barely open was
answered with a photograph of one substantially open. **That was in the build
the owner judged**, and it is the most likely reading of *"there is a small
difference, but there is no progress at all"* that day 14 could not see.

`widened x (1 + parted)`.

|---|---|---|

same value as a resting one"* — and kept the floor in the line below it.

engine nor the builder followed.

rest as a spread one.

measured by reading `mouthPucker` off the installed rungs with one tracker, in a

given** — `timestamp_of` says exactly that, two files away — so a run of stills
poisons the blendshapes. The same file reads **0.997 in a run of thirteen and
0.000 in a run of 268**. Every pucker number taken that way is noise.

Re-measured with a fresh tracker for each photograph, which is the only way to
read a still, the relationship the signed axis was built on holds:

| the rung's width | rungs | its `mouthPucker`, p50 |
|---|---|---|
| -0.40 to -0.15 | 22 | **0.943** |
| -0.15 to -0.05 | 45 | 0.173 |
| -0.05 to +0.05 | 57 | 0.007 |
| +0.05 to +0.50 | 144 | 0.004 |

**r(width, pucker) = -0.69.** The signed width axis *is* the rounding axis, which
is what this pass claimed; the claim stands on this measurement rather than on
the one it was published with. The rest of the pass — 48% of his speaking frames
below rest, 43% of the width discarded, thirteen rungs in one bag — never
depended on a blendshape.

### What is still out of reach, and what he was right about

**Th cannot be measured at all.** MediaPipe's 52 blendshapes contain no tongue,
so a tongue between the teeth is invisible to this tracker. That shape can only
ever arrive by luck.

**And his screenshots are the right next step**, for the other problem: the
median step between consecutive photographs is 0.106 to 0.147 where the grid's
own spacing is 0.058. That is density, and density is material. It comes after
this, not before: the signed axis changes where every frame is filed, so a build
done first would have to be thrown away.

## Day 16, fourth and fifth passes — a cross-fade that was wrong, and the hold that was the passivity

**Two things happened here and the first one is mine.**

### The cross-fade, and why my own measurement did not catch it

Between the third pass and this one, the two nearest rungs were mixed rather
than one being shown whole — the turn ladder's trick, on the argument that the
grid is the same density. It shipped, and it was reverted within the hour. The
owner: *"The problem is still the same. Now the model is passive. It does not
follow the mouth often when talking ... before it followed more often."*

`_blend_sources` composes **by region**, so two mouth rungs are averaged against
each other over the same pixels — and a mouth photograph, in this file's own
words, *"differs from what is under it mostly in texture"*. The average ghosts
the lip edge and the teeth. It tracked geometrically and went soft as a picture,
and soft reads as not moving.

**The measurement that should have caught it:**

| | one photograph | cross-faded |
|---|---|---|
| `231503` | gain 0.91, r 0.94, travels 94% | **gain 0.62, r 0.75, travels 77%** |
| `214327` | travels 90% | travels 84% |

A third of the amplitude gone, on the capture he made just before saying it had
gone passive. The report at the time was that the picture's step per frame had
fallen 2x to 43x, and it never asked whether the picture still *travelled*.
**A metric that rewards small steps is maximised by a mouth that does not move.**
`_nearest_rung` had already written the rule down — *"One rung, not two ... what
the owner objected to was two photographs on screen at once"* — and it was
overridden on a density argument that was not the point.

### And then the hold went, which is what he had been describing all along

Every other ladder keeps the rung in hand until a challenger is clearly nearer.
Speech kept it at **four times** their margin, since 2026-08-26, plus
`_SPEECH_RELEASE` added earlier the same day to stop that margin going stale.
Both are gone. Measured on all five captures, through the engine:

| | changes a second | gain | travels | error p50 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `201745` | 0.3 → 0.3 | 0.93 → 0.93 | 88% → 88% | 0.021 |
| `201806` | 7.5 → **11.8** | 0.96 → 0.96 | 97% → 97% | 0.035 |
| `201814` | 0.6 → **1.8** | 1.02 → 1.02 | 88% → 88% | 0.030 |
| `214327` | 3.2 → **5.7** | 0.96 → **0.98** | 90% → **94%** | 0.023 |
| `231503` | 2.8 → **4.4** | 0.91 → **0.99** | 94% → **100%** | 0.020 |

**It follows between 1.6 and 3 times as often, and every other number moves the
right way at the same time** — which is what says the hold was a fault and not a
trade. A rung held until the mouth is 0.15 away from it is two or three rungs
past its neighbour by the time it changes: the mouth moved late, and then jumped.

The flicker the margin was added for is real and is now paid in single-frame
runs — 39 of 81 becoming 91 of 126 on `201806`. **That is the trade he keeps
choosing:** he has called the mouth passive three times and flickering once.

### What is left is the grid, and it is material rather than code

With no hold at all the median step is still **0.106 to 0.147** where the grid's
own nearest-neighbour spacing is **0.058**. Along the line his mouth actually
travels the neighbours are two or three spacings away — half the grid sits at
widenings he never reaches, and the parting band he lives in is thinly filled.
**No choosing rule can close that; more photographs can.** `build_speech_ladder`
takes `--step`, currently 0.1, and the 1080p 9:16 `speech` clip has not been
generated yet.

Second candidate, cheaper and untried: **interpolate the geometry without
interpolating the pixels.** Show one photograph, crisp, but place its lips at a
position interpolated between the two nearest rungs. The shape would move
continuously and there would be no double exposure, because only one photograph
is ever on screen. It is the cross-fade's intent without the cross-fade's cost.

## Day 16, second half — he pressed the button, and the width axis was the fault

**Three captures**, 20:17:45, 20:18:06 and 20:18:14 on 2026-08-27, 300 frames
each, a face detected on every one of the 900. The first live test where a
complaint arrived as data rather than as a sentence. `scripts/replay_capture.py`
replays them; `captures/moment-20260827-*` holds them.

**They found something bigger than the hold did.**

### The resting mouth was learned as the narrowest mouth recently made

`_widened` — the grid's second axis, and the gate that decides whether the mouth
region is taken from the portrait at all — measures the mouth against a *rest*
learned per face. Rest was :meth:`_Neutral.relative_expression`: down to a new
low at :data:`_FLOOR_FALL` in a quarter of a second, back up at
:data:`_FLOOR_RISE` over twenty. **A running minimum.**

That is the right estimator for a signal that only rises from rest, and the
mouth is not one. **A mouth is narrower than rest every time it rounds an "oo"
or an "o"**, several times a sentence, and the floor parks there and stays for
twenty seconds — longer than anyone sits still.

His resting mouth is 0.57 of an eye span across. What the three captures found:

| | rest, as learned | the width axis, lips *together* | the grid's foot |
|---|---|---|---|
| `moment-...-201745` | **0.476** | **0.153** | open on **99%** of them |
| `moment-...-201806` | **0.409** | 0.227 | open on 88% |
| `moment-...-201814` | 0.539 | 0.065 | closed, correctly |

**The first capture is the defect entire.** He is sitting with his mouth shut
and the engine reads him as three quarters of the way into a closed smile
(a closed smile is 0.2 on this axis). So `_SPEECH_WIDTH_START` never closes, and
a widened photograph — `speech@75`, a mouth open 0.00 and widened 0.12 — is
held over his closed mouth at full opacity for **9.6 seconds**.

The third capture is the same engine with a floor that happened to land near his
rest, and it behaves. It is the control, and it is why this is the estimator and
not the axis.

**`test001.mkv` is no longer on the machine** — not on the desktop, not anywhere

### The measurement: the photograph is not lagging, it is stationary

The grid is chosen by nearest rung on two axes, and the rung in hand is kept
until a challenger is `_SPEECH_HOLD` = **0.20** nearer. Over those eight seconds,
against the installed 67-rung grid:

| | |
|---|---|
| stretches where the photograph did not change at all | 26 |
| his own parting moved, while it did not, at the median | **0.24** |
| and at worst | **0.46** |
| the longest single photograph | **800 ms** |
| photographs used, of the 67 installed | **14** |
| parting error against the driver, p50 | 0.103 |
| follow | gain 0.84, r 0.90 |

**0.46 of the parting axis is nearly half the way from closed lips to teeth
showing.** For 800 ms — a whole word — the mouth on screen was one photograph
while his own mouth crossed half its range. That is his sentence, arrived at
from the other end: it is not that the mouth lags, it is that it *stops*.

### Why a margin alone cannot see this

`_SPEECH_HOLD` asks one question — *is a challenger clearly nearer than the rung
in hand?* While a mouth walks away from its rung one syllable at a time, every
rung it passes is nearer by **less than the margin**, so the answer is no on
every single frame, and the sum of a hundred noes is a photograph that never
changes. Nothing in the test was watching how far wrong the photograph on screen
had become.

Day 15 set the margin to four times the other ladders' for a real reason — the
first version of this ladder changed photograph 7.6 times a second and held one
for a single frame on 28 runs of 62, and the owner saw it: *"the transition
between the mouth photographs is not smooth either."* The margin is right about
flicker. It was missing a ceiling.

### The fix, and what it costs

**`_SPEECH_RELEASE` = 0.15.** The incumbent keeps the region while it is clearly
nearest *and* while it is still within this far of the mouth being made. Same
eight seconds, through the engine's own `_nearest_speech`:

| | before | after |
|---|---|---|
| his mouth moved while the photograph froze, worst | **0.46** | **0.25** |
| and at the median | 0.24 | **0.11** |
| longest single photograph | 800 ms | **500 ms** |
| parting error, p50 | 0.103 | **0.037** |
| parting error, p95 | 0.227 | **0.132** |
| follow | gain 0.84, r 0.90 | **gain 0.96, r 0.98** |
| photographs used of 67 | 14 | **23** |
| changes of photograph | 4.2 a second | 6.6 a second |
| single-frame runs | 6 of 34 | 14 of 53 |

The last two rows are the price and they are the whole argument for a ceiling
rather than a smaller margin. **Dropping `_SPEECH_HOLD` to 0.05 buys the same
following and costs twice as much flicker** — 8.6 changes a second and 30
single-frame runs of 69, against 6.6 and 14 of 53. A 33 ms flash is what he
objected to; those are the runs that produce it.

### What was tried and does not work here

**Smoothing the measurement instead**, which is what the brows do and what
`_Neutral.smooth` was written for. Measured over the same window at rates 0.6,
0.4 and 0.3, it takes the amplitude off the movement without buying smoothness:
at 0.4 the follow falls to gain 0.79, r 0.90 and the changes stay at 9.2 a
second. A brow drifts; a mouth is fast, and a low-pass on a fast signal is just
a smaller mouth.

   clip reported 0 of 22 roles while every frame it needed sat in the file: no
   resting face to measure against, and a blur floor taken from the still
   frontal frames. Now 7 of 22 from that clip, and the ladder below.
3. **The eyes never actually closed** (the owner's standing complaint). A squint
   took half of every blink, and a *held* closure read 0.14 on `eyeBlink` — so
   two seconds of shut eyes rendered as a squint. Closure is measured on the
   eyelids now, which only became possible once the lid ring existed.
4. **A turn is chosen rather than averaged** (backlog 17). Thirty photographs
   at measured angles from −47° to +56°, one per three degrees; the engine takes
   the two that bracket the head and clamps past the ends. Seven times the turn
   coverage for 0.30 ms.

### The scope decision — made 2026-08-15

The owner's answer, and it removes most of what was threatening the schedule:

- **No licence system and no payments.** The application is free to use. There
  is nothing to check, no accounts, no entitlements.
- **A kill switch is still wanted** — the owner wants to be able to turn the
  application off when they choose.
- **The download site they will build themselves**, separately. Not our work.
- **Avatar encryption: dropped.**

What that leaves is far smaller than the two days a licence server was costed
at. A kill switch with no licensing behind it is a static file on any host —
GitHub raw, a plain web page, anything — that the application reads and obeys.
No server to write, nothing to keep running, nothing to administer.

Three things have to be right, and they are design decisions rather than code:

1. **It must fail open.** If the check cannot be reached, the application keeps
   working. A network blip that ends a live call is a worse failure than a
   delayed shutdown, and the owner can wait an hour for the switch to take.
2. **It must not block the UI.** The check belongs off the frame path entirely,
   at startup and then occasionally, never in the loop.
3. **It is a courtesy switch, not enforcement.** Anyone technical can block the
   address or patch the binary. It stops an ordinary user; it does not stop a
   determined one, and it should not be sold to the client as though it does.

Remaining before delivery: the kill switch, `.exe` packaging, and quality.

### Buying a video generator — what to check, in this order

The owner is paying for one, so this is what decides whether the money buys
anything usable. Ordered by what kills the plan outright.

1. **Image-to-video, starting from `portrait.png`.** The single most important
   one and the easiest to overlook. A tool that only generates from a text
   prompt produces a *different person*, and every photograph, the manifest and
   the tuning already built around this identity become worthless. The avatar
   must be driven from the portrait that already exists.
2. **No watermark at the tier being bought.** Not "removable" — absent. A mark
   across the face makes a frame useless as a source, and removing one is
   normally a licence breach in exactly the commercial case this is for.
3. **Commercial use, output owned by the user.** The shape to look for is
   OpenAI's: *"you own the Output; we assign to you all our right, title, and
   interest, if any"*.
4. **1080p or better**, and the face filling much of the frame. Frames are cut
   from this and the current portrait is 1024×1024 — a small face in a 720p clip
   is a downgrade, not an upgrade.
5. **Length and identity stability.** One long clip beats several short ones:
   every restart is a chance for the face to drift. Whatever arrives, every
   frame goes through `scripts/check_avatar_photos.py` before it is used.

### Queued here, in order

1. ~~The wedge above a blinking eye~~ (backlog 15) — **fixed 2026-08-15.** The
   eyelid ring was half of it; the other half was that MediaPipe reports the
   whole iris circle including the part behind the lid, so on a blink the mesh
   claimed a 30-pixel iris inside a 13-pixel eye. Rendered before and after on
   the recorded clip. Costs 1.18 ms of the ~2.5 ms headroom.
2. ~~`INTER_AREA` before the mesh warp~~ (backlog 9) — **done**, and it moved

3. **A 4K source renders *softer* than a small one** (backlog 16). Open,
   measured, not diagnosed. Until it is, more 4K clips buy coverage but not
   sharpness.
4. **If a video arrives:** extract frames, run every one through
   `scripts/check_avatar_photos.py`, and then the real prize — switch the engine
   from *blending* sources to *selecting* the nearest. Dense coverage is what
   makes that possible, and it removes cross-fade ghosting by construction
   rather than by clamping.

## Verified facts — do not re-derive these

**Hardware** (`scripts/check_gpu.py`)
- RTX 5070 Ti, 16303 MiB, compute capability 12.0 (`sm_120`), driver 610.62
- i7-14700KF, 32 GB, Windows 11

**Toolchain** (B001)
- `torch 2.11.0+cu128` includes `sm_120` kernels and a real kernel launch
  succeeds. The Blackwell support problem reported through 2025 is resolved.
- `onnxruntime-gpu 1.28.0` exposes **both** CUDA and TensorRT providers.
- A portrait-shaped conv stack at 256×256 runs in 1.83 ms fp32 / 1.33 ms fp16.

**Camera** (B002)
- Sony ZV-E10 over USB Streaming: **1280×720 @ 29.77 fps**, median interval
  33.19 ms, jitter 4.4 ms.
- **DirectShow is required.** Media Foundation cannot open this device.
- **NTSC/PAL gates the frame rate.** PAL offers only 25 fps; NTSC gives 30.
  Global camera setting, separate from the recording format.
- 720p is a limit of the camera's USB Streaming, not the cable. Accepted —
  the engine works at 256×256 internally.

**Face tracking** (B003)
- MediaPipe Face Landmarker `1.0.0`: **6.30 ms mean / 6.93 ms p95** per frame
  over 1747 frames, 100% detection, 478 landmarks + 52 blendshapes.
- **No GPU delegate exists on the Windows Python wheel** — it is compiled out.
  Tracking is CPU/XNNPACK, which leaves the GPU entirely free for the engine.
- MediaPipe emits **no confidence score**. Detection is binary.
- Colour conversion must use OpenCV, not numpy: 0.45 ms vs 2.90 ms per frame.
- Head-pose signs are verified, not assumed. Two of three were wrong initially.
- **Full pipeline with the real tracker: 29.50 fps sustained**, tracking 6.37 ms
  inside the application versus 6.30 ms standalone — integration costs nothing
  measurable.

**Avatar engine** (day 4)
- **Landmark-warp engine**: the portrait is deformed per frame onto the tracked
  face. No neural network, no model weights, **no licensing risk**.
- **13.8 ms mean / 17.7 ms p95** in ordinary use; 15.2 / 19.2 in the worst case
  where the expression changes every single frame — six sources, and 97 mesh
  points as the mesh then stood. It now carries **139 points and 229 triangles**
  (the full lip contours, both irises, and since day 8 the full eyelid rings),
  and with the owner's 20 sources costs **20.42 ms mean / 22.51 ms p95** over
  1817 frames of the recorded clip.
- First version cost 42 ms at p95 — over budget. The fix was one warp per
  triangle instead of two, and working inside the face's bounding box. A second
  round cached the source blend against weights quantised to 0.02 and painted
  the mouth interior inside the mouth's own box rather than the whole face's.
- **Multi-source: 22 photograph roles**, selected and blended per frame from
  blendshapes the tracker already measures and the head pose B003 verified. No
  thresholds, no state machine; every role is optional. Six supplied so far.
- **Seam fixed** by low-frequency relighting rather than Poisson blending, which
  measured 21–29 ms and did not fit. The forehead is now covered.
- Not photorealistic synthesis. LivePortrait remains the quality target and is
  still blocked on the weight-licence question.
- Five quality problems were observed on real use and recorded with causes in
  `AVATAR_QUALITY_BACKLOG.md`. One is fixed, three became supply problems that
  the 22-role set answers, one is engine-side and done. **A periodic flicker had
  three causes addressed on day 4; the owner is verifying whether a fourth
  remains.**

**Virtual camera** (day 5, B004)
- `pyvirtualcam 0.15.0` behind `VirtualCameraSink`, writing to the **OBS Virtual
  Camera** device. OBS must be *installed*; it does not need to be running.
- **1.44 ms mean / 1.54 ms p95** per 720p BGR frame. RGB placeholder frames cost
  0.65 ms more because they are converted first.
- Full pipeline sustained **29.65 fps** with publishing on, 593 frames, **zero
  dropped by the sink** — measured without an avatar loaded, so it does not yet
  include the warp engine's 13.8 ms.
- **GPL-2.0, verified from the installed package's own metadata. It cannot
  ship** — copyleft would oblige giving the client source, and source is all
  anyone needs to remove the licence server's kill switch. The committed default
  stays `null`; this machine selects it in gitignored `configs/local.toml`.
- Publishing is a **checkbox in the window**, off until asked for, and it can be
  switched during a call.

**Budget** — every line from one run, 596 frames, 20 sources (B004)
```
33.6 ms  frame interval the camera actually delivers (29.77 fps)
 6.9 ms  face tracking (p95 8.1), CPU
22.1 ms  avatar animation (p95 25.2), CPU  <- 6.8 of it is the source blend
 0.3 ms  rendering (p95 0.4)
 1.8 ms  virtual camera output (p95 2.7)
-------
31.1 ms  spent, ~2.5 ms headroom
```

**The blend cache stopped working.** It is keyed on weights quantised to 0.02;
with six sources it almost always hit, with 12.1 active per frame it hits 2.3%
of the time and the blend is rebuilt on 97.7% of frames. Capping the source
count was measured and rejected — the weight beyond the top six averages 0.59
of the total, so cheap caps change the picture and safe caps save little. The
lever is the cache and the per-pass cost. **First task for day 6.**
